How Much Allowance Should I Give My Kid, by Age?

Commonly cited ranges run roughly $0.50 to $1.00 per task for ages 6 to 8, $1.00 to $2.50 for ages 9 to 11, and $2.00 to $5.00 for ages 12 and up, drawn from parenting and chore-chart content rather than a single authority. There's no single correct number for any age. What matters more than the exact dollar figure is consistency: whether it's paid on a predictable schedule and not docked for an occasionally missed chore, which is closer to what the child-development research on motivation actually supports.

These figures are aggregated from multiple parenting and chore-chart sources rather than a single study, and they vary by household income, region, and how many tasks are expected. Treat them as a starting reference point, not a rule.

Per-task vs. a fixed weekly amount

Paying per completed task ties the dollar amount directly to effort and is easy for a young child to understand, but it also means allowance fluctuates week to week and can feel like piecework rather than a stable amount to plan around. A fixed weekly allowance, independent of exactly how many chores got done, is simpler to budget on both sides and is the more common structure once a family settles into a routine, with occasional bonuses layered on for extra effort rather than the base amount itself changing.

What the research says about docking pay for a missed chore

A body of child-development research generally favors paying a fixed allowance that isn't reduced when a required chore is missed, using non-punitive consequences or separate bonuses instead. The reasoning, drawn from research on intrinsic motivation (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999) and children's helping behaviour (Warneken & Tomasello, 2008), is that docking pay reframes a household contribution as a transaction that can be bought out of, which tends to undermine the habit rather than reinforce it. Other research on family chore practices (Rossmann, 2002; White et al., 2019) informs the broader picture of how chore participation develops over childhood. This is one defensible position, not a universal rule — plenty of families dock allowance for missed chores and it works fine for them.

How the amount should change as a child gets older

The jump in these ranges by age band tracks two things at once: kids take on more, and harder, tasks as they get older, and they also want more independent spending money as their social life and interests expand. A common, low-friction pattern is to hold the amount steady between ages rather than adjusting it every few months, then revisit it once — around a birthday, paired with a track record of the current chores being handled consistently — rather than renegotiating on demand.

Frequently asked questions

How much allowance should a 6-year-old get?

Commonly cited figures run about $0.50 to $1.00 per completed task at this age, though many families use a smaller flat weekly amount instead. There's no fixed correct number; consistency in paying it matters more than the exact figure.

How much allowance is normal for a 9 to 11-year-old?

Roughly $1.00 to $2.50 per task is a commonly cited range, or an equivalent flat weekly amount in that ballpark. Households vary widely based on income and local cost of living.

Should allowance go up automatically with age?

Most families increase it gradually as kids take on bigger responsibilities and want more independent spending money, rather than on a fixed schedule. Tying an increase to a birthday plus a track record of consistency is a common, low-friction approach.

Should I dock allowance for a missed chore?

Research on intrinsic motivation generally favors not docking a fixed allowance for an occasional missed chore, using a separate consequence or bonus system instead, because docking pay can turn a household responsibility into something that feels transactional. It's a defensible stance, not the only correct one — some families do dock pay and it works for them.
