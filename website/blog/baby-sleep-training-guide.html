Sleep training, calmly: what the evidence actually says

By Kinrows· August 8, 2026· 8 min read

Almost no part of family life comes with as much loud, contradictory advice as baby sleep. One book says never let them cry. The next says a few nights of crying is the kindest thing you can do. Meanwhile it is 2am, you have read four forum threads on your phone, and none of them agree. Here is what the research actually supports, in plain language, and how to turn it into something you can hold steady.

This is educational guidance, not medical advice. Every baby is different — talk to your pediatrician before you start, and stop if your child is unwell.

Safe sleep comes before any method

Whatever you decide about training, the safe-sleep basics do not change. They come from the American Academy of Pediatrics' 2022 policy statement and the NIH's Safe to Sleep guidance, and they are worth re-reading even if you think you know them:

  • On their back, for every sleep, until age one — including preterm babies and babies with reflux.
  • A firm, flat, level surface. Never an incline, never a soft or hammock-like surface.
  • Bare is best: no pillows, blankets, bumpers, soft toys, pods, nests, or weighted swaddles.
  • Room-share for at least the first six months — baby in your room, on their own sleep surface — but never bed-share.
  • Avoid overheating. Head uncovered, sleep sack instead of loose bedding, room around 16–20°C.
  • Stop swaddling at the first sign of rolling, and don't use car seats or swings for routine sleep.

None of the methods below are worth anything if the sleep space isn't safe. Start there.

Under four months, you are not sleep training

Newborn sleep is driven by hunger and a body clock that hasn't finished wiring itself. There is no method that changes that, and the sleep-duration consensus published by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine deliberately starts at four months, because below that age the experts could not agree on a normal range. Wide variation is the normal range.

So the first few months are about rhythm rather than training. Feed on demand, day and night. Keep days bright and social, and keep night feeds quiet, dim, and boring, so the difference starts to register. Follow wake windows — roughly 45 to 90 minutes at this age — so you catch tiredness before it turns into overtiredness. And when you can, put your baby down drowsy but awake, so falling asleep in their own space starts to feel ordinary. That last habit is the one that pays off later.

The routine is worth more than the method

If you take one thing from this article, take this. A study of 10,085 mothers found a straightforward dose-response relationship between a nightly bedtime routine and sleep outcomes: the more nights per week a family ran the same wind-down, the faster children fell asleep, the less they woke, and the more total sleep everyone got. More consistency, better sleep — at every age studied, in every country studied.

A routine doesn't need to be elaborate. Bath, book, feed, bed. The same order, roughly the same time, every night. What makes it work is that it is boring and predictable: it tells a small nervous system what is coming next, and it does the settling work before you ever get to the cot.

Screens off about an hour before bed once you're past the baby stage, and a consistent wake-up time — even after a rough night — protects the body clock more than a lie-in repairs it.

The methods that have evidence behind them

The behavioural sleep interventions with real trial support are fewer than the internet suggests. These are the main ones:

  • Bedtime fading (4+ months) — temporarily set bedtime to when your baby actually falls asleep, then move it 10–15 minutes earlier every few nights toward the time you want. Very little crying; excellent for bedtime resistance.
  • Graduated extinction, often called Ferber or check-and-console (4–6+ months) — put baby down awake; if they fuss, wait a set interval before a brief, calm check without picking up, lengthening the interval each time. The strongest evidence base of any method, and it usually works within three to seven nights.
  • Pick-up / put-down (4–7 months) — pick up to calm, put down awake as soon as they settle, repeat. Low-cry but labour-intensive, and it stops working well once babies get big enough to protest the transfer.
  • The chair method, or gradual retreat (6+ months) — sit beside the cot until sleep comes, then move the chair a little further away every few nights until you're out of the room. Gentler, slower, and a good fit if a check-in approach feels wrong to you.
  • Full extinction (4–6+ months) — after a loving routine, put baby down awake and don't return until morning apart from scheduled feeds and safety checks. Fastest results, and the follow-up research shows no long-term harm — but only worth doing if it genuinely fits your family.
  • Silent return (2+ years) — when a toddler gets out of bed or calls out, walk them back calmly, with no talking and no eye contact, every single time. Pair it with a sticker chart for staying put.
  • The bedtime pass (from about 2.5–3 years) — give your child one pass they can trade for a single sanctioned get-up or request after lights-out, ignore further bids, and let an unused pass earn a small reward in the morning. For this age it has the best evidence of anything: extinction-level results with far less crying.

Two things matter far more than which one you choose. First, pick one and give it a consistent one to two weeks — switching methods every few nights is what prolongs the crying. Second, both caregivers have to run it the same way. A method applied consistently by two people beats a better method applied inconsistently by one.

Is it harmful? What the follow-up studies found

This is the question that keeps parents up as much as the baby does, so it deserves a straight answer. A 2016 randomised trial in Pediatrics (Gradisar and colleagues) tested graduated extinction and bedtime fading against a control group and measured infants' stress hormones and attachment security directly. It found no adverse effects on either. A five-year follow-up of a larger Australian trial (Price and colleagues, 2012, also in Pediatrics) looked at children's emotional health, behaviour, sleep, stress, and their relationship with their parents at age six, and found no lasting differences between the trained and untrained groups.

The honest summary: the evidence says these approaches work in the short term and don't appear to cause harm in the long term. It does not say you must use them. Plenty of families decide the trade-off isn't for them, and that is a legitimate choice — not a failure.

Age by age, roughly

Sleep changes shape as children grow, and most "it stopped working" moments are really a phase change:

  • 0–3 months. Safe sleep, day-night rhythm, wake windows. Expect 14–17 hours across many short stretches, and night waking as standard.
  • 4–6 months. The classic window to begin. Set the bedtime routine, put baby down awake, pick a method. Move toward two or three predictable naps. The four-month regression is real; hold the routine through it.
  • 6–12 months. Long stretches are now developmentally possible and night feeds are often no longer needed (confirm with your pediatrician). Naps consolidate to about two. Separation anxiety around 8–10 months can look like a step backwards.
  • 12–18 months. Down to one longer midday nap, with more bedtime independence to negotiate. Firm, loving limits on stalling. Expect a brief 18-month wobble.
  • 18 months – 3 years. Imagination and boundary-testing arrive together. Front-load the "one more" requests into the routine — water, hug, bathroom — and hold the nap; dropping it early usually makes nights worse. Delay the big-kid bed until around three.
  • 3–4 years. A mostly independent sleeper, with the last nap on its way out. When it goes, move bedtime earlier to protect 10–13 hours. Bedtime pass for stalling.

When it isn't working

Sudden regressions almost always have a cause: teething, illness, travel, a developmental leap, or a nap that's outlived its usefulness. Ride them out on the routine you already have rather than rebuilding it — most settle within a week or two. A too-late bedtime is the most commonly missed culprit, because overtiredness looks a lot like under-tiredness from the outside; earlier is often the fix.

And if you've been consistent for two weeks and nothing has moved, that is a conversation with your pediatrician, not a sign that you need a seventh method.

Frequently asked questions

What age can you start sleep training?
Most formal training starts around four months, once sleep cycles and the body clock have matured. Before that, focus on safe sleep, day-night rhythm, and a gentle wind-down. Check with your pediatrician first.

Is sleep training harmful to babies?
The trials say no. Gradisar et al. (2016, Pediatrics) found no adverse effect on infant stress hormones or attachment; Price et al. (2012, Pediatrics) found no differences five years on in behaviour, emotions, or the parent-child relationship.

Which method works best?
There is no single winner. Graduated extinction and bedtime fading have the strongest infant evidence; the chair method and pick-up/put-down are gentler and slower; the bedtime pass is the standout for children of about three and up. Consistency beats method choice.

How long does it take?
Most families see a clear change in three to seven nights, and give any one method a fair one to two weeks. Interruptions from teething, illness, and travel are normal.

Does a bedtime routine really matter that much?
Yes — it's the single highest-value habit. Mindell et al. (2015), across 10,085 mothers, found that the more consistently a family ran a nightly routine, the faster children fell asleep, the less they woke, and the more sleep everyone got.

You do not need to get this perfect. Safe sleep, a boring routine you can repeat on your worst day, one method held steady for a fortnight, and two parents running the same plan will carry most families through. Everything else is detail.
