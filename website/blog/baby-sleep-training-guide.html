Sleep training a baby: what the evidence actually says

By Kinrows· August 8, 2026· 7 min read

Nobody makes good decisions at three in the morning. That is the real problem with baby sleep: the advice is loud, contradictory, and mostly arrives when you are too tired to weigh it. This is a calm walk through what the research actually supports — what to do before four months, what to do after, and how to keep track of it without turning your nights into a spreadsheet.

This is general information, not medical advice. Every baby is different. Talk to your pediatrician before you start anything, and stop if your child is unwell.

Safe sleep comes before everything else

No sleep strategy is worth anything if the sleep space is not safe. These rules hold at every age and in every phase, and they are the one part of this article that is not negotiable:

  • Baby sleeps on their back, every sleep, until their first birthday — including preterm babies and babies with reflux.
  • A firm, flat, level surface: a crib, bassinet, or play yard. Never an incline, never something soft or hammock-shaped.
  • Bare is best. No pillows, blankets, bumpers, soft toys, pods, nests, or weighted swaddles in the sleep space.
  • Room-share for at least the first six months — your room, their own sleep surface — but never bed-share.
  • Avoid overheating. Keep the head uncovered, use a sleep sack rather than loose bedding, and keep the room comfortably cool.
  • Stop swaddling at the first sign of rolling. Don't use car seats or swings for routine sleep, and don't rely on monitors that claim to prevent SIDS.

This is the current AAP and NHS guidance, and it does not change because a method promises faster results.

Under four months, you are not training — you are building rhythm

Newborns cannot be sleep-trained, and it is a relief to know that. Their sleep is driven by hunger and a body clock that has not finished arriving. Waking through the night is not a problem to be solved at this age; it is what is supposed to be happening.

What does help in these first months is quieter than training. Feed on demand, day and night, and don't stretch a hungry newborn. Keep daytime bright and sociable and keep night feeds dim, quiet, and boring, so the difference between the two starts to register. Watch the wake windows — roughly 45 to 90 minutes at this age — because overtiredness makes everything harder. And when you can, put your baby down drowsy but awake, so falling asleep in their own bed starts to feel ordinary.

That is it. This phase is survival, not a project. Trade night shifts if there is anyone to trade with.

Around four months, the window opens

Somewhere near four months, most babies become capable of learning to fall asleep on their own — and, with a pediatrician's blessing, this is the classic window to begin. It is also when the four-month regression tends to land, which is confusing, because the thing that looks like everything falling apart is often the same maturation that makes training possible.

The single most important habit here is a consistent bedtime and a short, same-every-night routine: bath, book, feed, bed. Then put your baby down awake, so they get to practise the actual skill. Then pick one method and give it one to two consistent weeks. Chopping and changing between approaches is what drags the crying out.

The methods that hold up

There is no single winner here, which is good news — it means you can choose the one your family can actually sustain.

  • Bedtime fading (4 months+). Temporarily set bedtime to whenever your baby naturally falls asleep, then move it 10 to 15 minutes earlier every few nights toward the time you actually want. Low-crying, and strong trial evidence behind it.
  • Graduated extinction, often called Ferber or check-and-console (4–6 months+). Put your baby down awake; if they fuss, wait a set interval before a brief, calm check without picking them up, lengthening the interval each time. Usually works within three to seven nights.
  • Pick-up / put-down (4–7 months). Pick up to calm, then put down awake as soon as they settle, repeating as needed. Very low-cry, very labour-intensive, and best before around seven months.
  • The chair method, or gradual retreat (6 months+). Sit beside the crib until your baby sleeps, then move the chair a little further away every few nights until you are out of the room. Gentler, slower, and it asks for patience.
  • Full extinction, the one everyone calls "cry it out" (4–6 months+). After a loving routine, put your baby down awake and don't return until morning, aside from scheduled feeds and safety checks. Fastest results, and the follow-up research shows no long-term harm — but only worth doing if it genuinely fits your family.
  • The bedtime pass (about 3 years+, workable from 2½). Give your child one pass they can trade for a single sanctioned get-up or request after lights-out; ignore further bids, and let an unused pass earn a small reward in the morning. For this age group it has the strongest evidence of anything on this list — extinction-level results with far less crying.
  • Silent return (2 years+). When your child leaves the bed or calls out, walk them back calmly with no talking and no eye contact. Every time. Pair it with a sticker chart for staying put. Consistency is the whole mechanism.

Does sleep training harm children? The honest answer

This is the question that keeps parents awake more reliably than the baby does, so it deserves a straight answer.

In 2016, Gradisar and colleagues published a randomised controlled trial in Pediatrics comparing graduated extinction and bedtime fading against a control group. They measured infant stress hormones and parent-child attachment directly, and found no adverse effects from either method. Price and colleagues, also in Pediatrics, followed families five years on and found no lasting differences in children's emotions, their behaviour, or the parent-child relationship. A wider review of behavioural sleep interventions by Mindell and colleagues found the same pattern: these methods work, and the harm people fear has not shown up in the data.

That is a genuine reassurance, not a nudge. Plenty of families decide sleep training is not for them and that is an entirely reasonable call. But if you have been holding off out of a worry that you might be damaging something, the evidence does not support the worry.

The one lever with the most evidence behind it

If you take a single thing from this article, take this: a consistent bedtime and a short, predictable routine beats any particular method. Mindell and colleagues studied more than 10,000 families and found a dose-response relationship — the more nights a family kept the routine, the better the child slept and the fewer night wakings there were. More nights, better sleep, in a straight line.

It also costs nothing, works at every age, and quietly fixes problems you have not diagnosed yet. Before you go looking for a method, look at your last two weeks of bedtimes. If they range across an hour and a half, that is the thing to fix first.

Which is, of course, the catch. Almost nobody can tell you what their child's average bedtime was last week.

When it wobbles, and it will

Sleep is not a problem you solve once. Teething, illness, travel, and developmental leaps will all knock things sideways, usually for a week or two. Separation anxiety around eight to ten months brings new night waking that has nothing to do with your method. The 18-month regression is real. So is the nap transition that arrives just as everything had settled.

The move in all of these cases is the same: hold the routine steady and wait it out. Most regressions resolve on their own within a fortnight if the scaffolding stays put. What extends them is a fortnight of trying something different every night.

And if a dip holds for more than a couple of weeks, or arrives alongside a change in feeding or alertness, that is a conversation for your pediatrician — not a harder method.

Frequently asked questions

What age can you start sleep training?
Around four months, once the body clock and sleep cycles have matured. Before that, night waking is developmentally normal and the work is rhythm rather than training. Check with your pediatrician before starting, whatever the age.

Is sleep training harmful to a baby?
The best available evidence says no. A randomised controlled trial by Gradisar and colleagues found no adverse effects on infant stress hormones or attachment, and Price and colleagues found no lasting differences five years on.

Which sleep training method works best?
There is no single winner. Graduated extinction and bedtime fading both have strong trial evidence; the chair method and pick-up/put-down are gentler but slower; the bedtime pass is the strongest option for children of about three and over. Consistency matters more than the choice.

How long does sleep training take to work?
Usually three to seven nights for graduated or full extinction, two to three weeks for gentler methods. The hardest night is often the second or third rather than the first.

What is the most important thing for baby sleep?
A consistent bedtime and a short, same-order routine. The Mindell dose-response study of more than 10,000 families found that the more nights you keep it, the better your child sleeps. It is the highest-evidence, lowest-cost change available to you.

You do not need to pick the perfect method. Get the sleep space safe, get bedtime steady, choose one approach you can hold for a fortnight, and give it the consistency it needs. The rest is mostly patience — and knowing what your last two weeks actually looked like, rather than what 3am told you they did.
