How to decide whether to tie allowance to chores

Most child-development research favours paying a fixed allowance that's separate from required household chores, and using non-punitive bonuses for extra effort instead of docking pay for a missed chore. Docking pay tends to reframe a chore as a paid transaction rather than a household contribution, which research on intrinsic motivation suggests can undermine the habit rather than build it. A middle path — fixed allowance, chores expected regardless, optional bonuses for consistency — captures most of the benefit of both approaches.

Key takeaway: Pay the allowance regardless; use bonuses, not deductions, for extra effort.

Last reviewed 2026-09-15

The case for tying allowance to chores

The instinct makes sense: chores are work, and work is usually paid, so tying allowance to specific tasks feels like it teaches a direct, honest lesson about earning money. It also gives a parent a clean lever — no chore, no pay — that doesn't require a separate conversation about consequences every time something gets skipped. For some families, especially with older kids taking on real responsibilities, a per-task or points-based system is straightforward to run and easy for a child to understand.

The case against (what the research says about motivation)

The research most often cited on this question points the other way. Work on intrinsic versus extrinsic motivation — including Deci, Koestner and Ryan's 1999 analysis of reward effects on motivation — has long found that tying a reward too tightly to a task can shift how a person thinks about that task: from something done because it's expected or satisfying, to something done only because it's paid. Warneken and Tomasello's 2008 research on children's helping behaviour found a related pattern: young children are naturally inclined to help, and introducing material rewards for helping can reduce that inclination afterward. Applied to chores, the concern is that docking allowance for a missed chore teaches "this is a transaction I can opt out of by forfeiting pay," rather than "this is something our household does."

A middle path: fixed allowance plus optional bonuses

Most of the research-informed guidance in this space lands on a middle position rather than either extreme:

  • Chores are expected as part of being in the household, not optional paid tasks.
  • Allowance is paid on a fixed schedule regardless of one missed chore, so it isn't a per-task wage.
  • Optional bonuses reward consistency or extra effort — a good bedtime streak, taking on an extra task without being asked — without ever turning into a deduction.
  • Consequences for a skipped chore, if needed, are handled separately from money (a conversation, a redo), rather than through the allowance itself.

This isn't the only defensible approach — reasonable parents land in different places — but it's the one most consistent with the motivation research above.

How this changes by age

Age changes what the chore and the money mean, even under a fixed-allowance approach. Research on the development of chore participation, including Rossmann's 2002 findings and White et al.'s 2019 work on childhood chore involvement, generally supports starting simple, habit-level tasks early — well before allowance is a meaningful concept — and increasing both responsibility and allowance amount as a child gets older. A toddler's "chore" is about building a habit; a teenager's is closer to a real household responsibility, and the money attached to it can reasonably start to resemble a small, real budget rather than a token amount.

Frequently asked questions

Should you tie allowances to chores?

Most child-development research leans toward keeping them separate: pay a fixed allowance regardless of one missed chore, and use non-punitive bonuses for extra effort instead of docking pay. Tying pay too tightly to specific tasks can turn an expected household contribution into a transaction a child can simply opt out of by giving up the money.

What happens if a kid skips a chore — should you dock their allowance?

Research on intrinsic motivation generally advises against it. Docking pay reframes the chore as paid work rather than a household expectation, which can reduce the underlying willingness to help rather than reinforce it. A non-money consequence — a conversation, redoing the task — keeps the chore and the allowance as separate systems.

How much allowance is normal by age?

There's no single standard figure, and specific dollar amounts vary widely by household and region. What's more consistent across guidance is the shape: small, simple tasks with little or no pay at the youngest ages, increasing responsibility and allowance through the school years, and something closer to a real, larger allowance by the teen years.

What's the difference between a chore and a paid task?

A chore is generally treated as an expected part of being in the household, done regardless of payment. A paid task is closer to a one-off job — washing the car for a set amount, for example — that a family may choose to compensate separately from the regular allowance, without turning every regular chore into the same kind of transaction.
